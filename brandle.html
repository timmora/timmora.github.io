Overview

Brandle is a daily brand-guessing game with two lives. The first was one semester in a team of four, where I pitched the idea and built the frontend. The second started two years later, when I forked that repo and rebuilt it by myself. You guess the mystery brand and get color-coded feedback on five attributes: category, country, founding year, revenue, and employees. The game itself was never the hard part. The hard part was the data, and whether it was accurate enough that a close guess actually felt close.

Where it started

Brandle started as a class project for CS 222: Software Design Lab at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, and it very nearly didn't happen. Our assignment was to build an original full-stack application that was fun and interesting. I came in with a few pitches: a Spotify playlist generator based on your mood, a mental health journal with guided prompts, and a productivity app for students (which I did end up building later as Ordo, funnily enough). None of them really landed with my teammates, so we kept thinking.

At a later meeting I was playing my daily game of Wordle, and that's where Brandle came from. Everyone has a fuzzy picture of a company in their head: where it's from, how big it is, how long it's been around. Nobody ever checks that picture. A Wordle-style guessing game seemed like a fun way to do it. The team liked it, and we decided to build it.

Four people, one semester

We split the work the way most class teams do. Two teammates built the Node backend: an Express API, a Sequelize model on a local MySQL database, and the tests. One owned the brand dataset and wrote the insert scripts that loaded it. I owned the frontend. I set up the React project, laid out the guessing interface and navigation, wrote the first frontend tests, and reviewed and merged most of the frontend pull requests.

My biggest change that semester came in the final stretch. The game logic lived partly on the server, so every guess made a round trip just to find out which tiles should be green. I moved the logic into the frontend, simplified the backend down to serving brand data, and restyled every component in the same pass. We ended the semester with a working game. It had 90 brands, a random answer on every page load, and it ran only on localhost.

Picking it back up

Two years later, in April 2026, I forked the repo to see whether I could take Brandle past where the class left it. I was a very different developer by then, and the codebase felt like someone else's, even the parts I wrote. Recognizing my own components didn't help me remember why I'd written them that way. So before changing anything I read through it all again, found the spots where it quietly broke, and started pulling on those.

Where it runs

The class version needed a MySQL server, an Express API, and a React dev server running at once, with CORS pinned to one localhost port. I moved the data to Supabase and had the client read it directly, with row-level security on every table. Then I deleted the whole Node backend and deployed the frontend to Vercel. Three processes became zero servers to look after.

The puzzle

The original picked a new random brand every time the page loaded, and printed the answer to the console. That makes a fine demo but it's not a daily game. Now each day has a numbered puzzle counted from January 1, 2026, everyone gets the same brand, and your finished result is saved so a refresh can't give you a second try.

The feedback

Revenue was stored as a string like "$300B+", so it could only ever be an exact match or wrong, and none of the numbers had arrows. I added a revenue tier scale so revenue gets "close" feedback too, put higher/lower arrows on all three numeric fields, and made the thresholds depend on difficulty.

Streaks

The old streak went up on every win, even if your last game was a month ago. It now checks when you last played. It also has a weekly streak pass, because losing a 20-day streak to one bad brand felt like the game punishing you for playing.

The data

Every number in the original was typed in by hand once, and started going stale right away. I matched each brand to its Wikidata entry, and wrote a Supabase Edge Function that pulls fresh employee counts, revenue, and founding years. A GitHub Actions job runs it on the first of every month.

Highlights

  • 01

    Five signals per guess: green for exact, yellow for close, and arrows pointing higher or lower on the numbers. A warmer/colder indicator compares each guess to your last one, so you can tell if you're getting closer overall.

  • 02

    Three difficulties change more than the guess limit. Easy gives you 15 guesses and a ±20-year window on founding year. Hard gives you 6 guesses and ±5 years. The same brand can feel easy or brutal depending on those windows.

  • 03

    Hard mode makes you use what you've already found. Once category, country, or founding year turns green, every later guess has to match it.

  • 04

    Hints scale with difficulty: three on easy, two on normal, one on hard. On easy, every five wrong guesses automatically reveals a field you still haven't gotten right, so you don't get stuck.

  • 05

    Results share as a spoiler-free emoji grid or as an image drawn on a canvas. Stats are kept separately for each difficulty, and a signed-in player's games also save to Supabase. The leaderboard is built on that data.

Under the hood

The daily puzzle doesn't need a server. The puzzle number is just the count of days since January 1, 2026, and that number, modulo the brand count, picks the answer. Every player gets the same brand without a "today's puzzle" row, a cron job, or anything else to keep in sync. Unlimited and by-category modes simply pick at random.

The feedback algorithm checks all five attributes at once. Category and country are either right or wrong. Founding year, revenue tier, and employee count get graduated proximity feedback with arrows. Each difficulty sets its own tolerances, which is what really makes hard mode hard: a guess that shows yellow on normal shows gray on hard.

The data refresh was the part I cared about most. A one-time script searches Wikidata for each brand and prints SQL for me to check by hand, because the top result for "HP" isn't always the HP you mean. After that, the Edge Function sends one batched SPARQL query for every brand's employees, revenue, and founding date. It maps revenue onto the same tier scale the game uses, and writes the results back to the table.

"For a content-driven game, curation basically is the design work. Keeping brands consistent and accurate across five attributes took longer than building the game itself."

Deep dive — architecture

The browser talks to Postgres directly, and row-level security decides what it can read and write. The only server-side code is the monthly refresh, which needs the service role key and shouldn't run in anyone's browser.

Reflection

Brandle was the first project I built with a team, and the first full-stack app I helped ship. The class taught me the team side of software: branches, pull requests, splitting up work, and merging someone else's code into mine the night before a deadline. The fork taught me the other side: what it's like to maintain something by yourself, with nobody to ask why a file looks the way it does. Coming back to a codebase I had half-written and completely forgotten was humbling, and I'm glad I did it.

Most of the changes that mattered were small. A daily puzzle is a date and a modulo. A fair streak is one date comparison. What really decided whether the game felt good was the data. Founding years that are really acquisition dates, revenue that lags a year behind, a subsidiary listed as its parent company: any of those turns a smart guess into a wrong one, and the player blames the game. I ended up spending more time on data than on React components, and I think that was the right call.

The one thing I'd do differently is the daily seed. Stepping through the table in order is simple and needs no server, but anyone who knows the order can see tomorrow's brand coming. Shuffling the table with a fixed seed first would keep every benefit and remove the pattern. That's next on the list, along with actually putting the leaderboard in front of players.
