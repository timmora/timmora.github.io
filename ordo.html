Overview

Ordo is a full-stack productivity app built for students who have too much going on. It uses Claude AI to decompose assignments into subtasks, generates a daily briefing each morning, and surfaces the Daily Three — the three tasks that actually matter today — using a scheduling engine that models your real daily capacity: classes, focus windows, hard cutoffs, and all.

The Problem

Every student productivity app I tried had the same failure mode: it treated time as uniform. Monday at 10am looks identical to Thursday at 9pm in a calendar, but for a student those slots have completely different characters. One is before a 300-person lecture; the other is the night before a midterm. Generic to-do lists don't know the difference — and scheduling tools that ignore cognitive load and context aren't really scheduling tools at all.

The other problem was the gap between planning and starting. Students know they have a lot to do. The anxiety comes from not knowing what to do first, or how to fit everything around a schedule that changes week to week. I wanted Ordo to close that gap — not just list the tasks, but tell you what to do today and why.

Under the Hood

The scheduling engine is the core of Ordo. It takes a task graph — assignments decomposed by Claude into subtasks with estimated durations — and maps them into a weekly schedule, respecting course blocks, preferred focus windows, daily capacity limits, and hard deadlines. The algorithm runs on each login, so the plan adapts continuously as tasks are completed or delayed.

The AI layer has two distinct jobs. During onboarding, Claude parses syllabus PDFs and extracts the full course schedule — assignment dates, exam weights, recitation times — eliminating the input problem entirely. During operation, Claude writes the daily briefing: a personalized summary of today's priorities, yesterday's focus stats, and one concrete suggestion. The briefing is cached per user per day; the first request generates it, subsequent ones return the cache.

"The hard part wasn't the scheduling algorithm. It was making the Daily Three feel trustworthy enough that a student would actually follow it."

Reflection

The most interesting thing about building Ordo was discovering what the hard problem actually was. I expected the scheduling algorithm to be the challenge. It wasn't — constraint solvers are well-understood. The hard part was making the Daily Three feel trustworthy. If a student looks at their three tasks and thinks "that's not what I should be doing today," the whole product fails. Getting the priority logic right — especially for long-horizon projects that shouldn't surface until they need to — took more iteration than anything else.

One architectural decision I'm glad I made early: the journal is encrypted at rest with AES-256 per-user keys. The AI insights run on anonymized summaries, not raw entries. I could have cut that corner. I didn't.
