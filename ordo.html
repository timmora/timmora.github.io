Overview

Ordo takes your whole course load and turns it into an actual daily plan. It reads your syllabi, breaks assignments into smaller subtasks, and slots them into your day-to-day schedule — class blocks, focus windows, deadlines you're dreading. Every morning you get Today's Focus: the things you need to prioritize first today.

The problem

Every student productivity app I tried made the same mistake: it treats every hour like it's the same hour. Monday at 10am and Thursday at 9pm look identical on a calendar, but they're not even close. One's right before a 300-person lecture, the other's the night before a midterm. A regular to-do list has no idea, and if a scheduling tool doesn't get that either, it's failing to do its job.

The other problem is the disconnect between knowing you have a lot to do and actually starting. Every student already knows their list is long. The hard part is not knowing what to open first, especially when your schedule changes every week.

Highlights

  • 01

    AI decomposition plus a scheduler that actually respects your real week: class blocks, how much you can realistically focus, deadlines. It's not a to-do list, it's a plan that fits.

  • 02

    Upload your syllabus during onboarding and Claude pulls out every assignment and due date for you. No manual entry.

  • 03

    Every morning you get an AI briefing: what matters today, how your week's going, one actual suggestion. Cached per day so it's fast and doesn't burn through API credits.

  • 04

    Built-in Pomodoro timer that tracks time per subtask, logs sessions, and counts your streak. Keeps planning and actually doing close together.

  • 05

    Journal entries are encrypted at rest (Fernet, with key rotation). The AI briefing only ever sees your mood and energy, never what you actually wrote.

Under the hood

The scheduler is the core. It takes tasks already broken into subtasks with estimates, then fits them into your week around class blocks, focus windows, daily capacity, and hard deadlines. Google Calendar comes in over read-only OAuth, so anything already on there counts as busy. It reruns on every login, so the plan keeps adjusting as you finish things or fall behind.

Claude's other job is reading, not decomposing. During onboarding it parses syllabus PDFs and pulls out the whole course schedule — dates, weights, recitation times — so you don't type any of it in.

Deep dive — AI decomposition, cache first

A syllabus PDF can run 30k tokens. Paragraphs get scored against the task and only the relevant ones travel, Haiku handles most calls with Sonnet as fallback, and identical requests never reach the model twice.

Deep dive — architecture

Clients hit Postgres directly for basic reads and writes, locked down with row-level security. The backend only exists for what a client shouldn't be trusted with: calling Claude, running the scheduler across the whole task graph, syncing calendars.

Reflection

The most surprising part of building Ordo was realizing what the real hard problem was. I thought it'd be the scheduling algorithm. It wasn't — constraint solving is fairly solved. The hard part was trust. If you look at your Daily Three and think "that's not what I should be doing today," everything falls apart. Getting the priority logic right, especially for long-horizon projects that shouldn't surface until they need to, took more iteration than anything else.

One decision I'm glad I made early: the journal is encrypted at rest with key rotation, and the AI briefing only ever sees mood and energy, never what you wrote. I could've cut that corner.
