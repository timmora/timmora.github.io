Overview

HoloOrbits is a browser-based multiplayer simulation where secondary school students collaborate in real time to solve orbital mechanics challenges. As UX Lead on a 4-person team, I drove the redesign of the game's navigation, collaborative mechanics, and in-game feedback systems — shifting the experience from passive simulation to active, role-based problem-solving.

The Challenge

The original HoloOrbits was a single-player simulation. A student loaded it, moved through a series of orbital mechanics scenarios, and clicked through prompts. It worked, in the sense that it delivered content. But students weren't engaging with the physics — they were navigating past it.

Two things were broken. First, the experience was passive: prompts told students what was happening rather than putting them in a position to figure it out themselves. Second, it was solo: there was no reason to talk to anyone, no shared objective, no productive disagreement about what to do next.

The redesign question was: what does genuine collaboration look like in a physics simulation? Not just "two people can see the same screen," but shared decision-making with real stakes attached to the outcome.

"There's a difference between two students watching the same simulation and two students disagreeing about what to do next. We wanted the second one."

My Approach

I led UX from Figma to pilot, working closely with one teammate on the accuracy feedback system and Unity/WebGL implementation. My focus was on three interlocking problems: navigation redesign, multiplayer mechanic design, and in-game feedback.

Navigation: the original flow was linear and opaque — students didn't know where they were in the experience or what success looked like. I redesigned the navigation structure to make progress legible and the objective clear before students made any decisions.

Multiplayer mechanic: I designed a model where students have differentiated roles within each scenario — not just "two players controlling the same thing," but distinct responsibilities that require coordination to complete the objective. The design forces communication. Feedback system: passive prompts were replaced with a structured accuracy system giving students a response tied to the physics of what they just did — not a grade, but information: how close was the trajectory to the target orbit, and why.

Key Outcomes

  • 01

    Redesigned the experience for real classroom use — shifted the game from single-player simulation to multiplayer, where students work toward shared objectives with distinct, interdependent roles.

  • 02

    Replaced passive in-game prompts with a structured accuracy feedback system, giving students meaningful in-the-moment responses to their physics decisions rather than narrative descriptions of what is happening.

  • 03

    Ran a structured pilot with college students, gathering feedback that validated the collaborative mechanics and drove a final round of refinements before handoff.

The Pilot

We ran a structured pilot test with college students — not our target secondary school audience, but close enough to validate the core mechanics and surface friction in the new collaboration model.

We structured the session around specific hypotheses: would students understand the differentiated roles without explicit instruction? Would the accuracy feedback system prompt them to adjust their approach or ignore it? Were there points in the flow where both players stalled?

The results were mostly positive and useful in specific ways. Students understood the roles faster than expected once they saw the objective screen. The accuracy feedback prompted visible discussion — students turned to each other to interpret the numbers — which was exactly the behavior we were designing for. The main friction was in the transition between scenarios, which we addressed in the final round of changes.

Reflection

The moment in the pilot that made the semester worth it was watching two students debate which trajectory correction to apply. They were wrong about the physics, but they were genuinely disagreeing about it — treating it as a real problem with a real answer that mattered. That shift from passive player to active collaborator was the whole design goal.

Working on an educational game pushed me to think about engagement differently. In a consumer app, engagement means return visits and session length. In a classroom simulation, engagement means whether a student is actually thinking. Designing for genuine cognitive engagement — not just satisfying UI — was a different kind of design problem, and one I found genuinely compelling.
